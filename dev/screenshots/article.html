On reading

The case for reading with a pen in your hand

Paper taught us to argue with a text. The screen took the argument away. It is worth taking it back.

By Ada Merriweather · 6 September 2026 · 9 min read

A reader who holds a pen reads differently. The pen is a promise to respond, and the promise changes how closely the eye attends. This essay argues that the habit survived paper and can survive the screen, if the screen lets it.

Three habits are worth keeping: circling what puzzles, underlining what persuades, and tracing a line to keep a tired eye moving.

My grandmother's books are unreadable. Not because the print has faded but because she answered every page. Pencil brackets march down the margins. Question marks stand beside claims she doubted. A whole paragraph of a novel is crossed out with a single stroke and the word no. She was not defacing the books. She was reading them.

I inherited the habit without the books. On paper I still circle, underline and scribble. On a screen I do none of it, and I noticed only recently that I also remember less of what I read there. The two facts are related, and the relation is the subject of this essay.

What the pen does for the eye

A pen slows the hand, and a slow hand slows the eye to the speed of understanding. Reading is not a single act but a negotiation between the speed at which we can recognise words and the speed at which we can hold their meaning. Left alone, recognition wins. The eye skates. A pen resting on the line is a small weight that keeps the eye from skating, and a mark at the end of a sentence is a receipt for having understood it.

There is a second, humbler service. Tired eyes lose their place. On a dense page the same line gets read twice and the next one not at all. A finger under the line, or a pen drawn along it, is the oldest reading aid there is, and no one has improved on it. Children are taught to stop doing it, which is a pity, because the readers who kept the habit are the ones who can still read a dense page at midnight.

The margin is where the reader stops being an audience and becomes a correspondent.

What the screen took away

The screen did not take the pen away on purpose. It simply never had one. Text on a screen is a picture of text, redrawn sixty times a second, and a mark on a picture has nowhere to live. When the page scrolls, the mark must scroll. When a paragraph moves because an advert loaded above it, the mark must move too. These are hard problems, and for a long time the easiest answer was to offer a highlighter that turned words a dull yellow and called it annotation.

The result is a generation of readers who read a great deal and mark nothing. The reading is not worse in every way. It is faster, wider and more searchable. But it is shallower, and the shallowness is not a moral failing. It is what happens when the hand has nothing to do.

Taking it back

The pen is back, more or less. A stylus on a tablet is a good pen, better than most pencils. What is missing is permission: a page that lets the pen draw wherever it lands and keeps the drawing where it was made. That is a smaller ask than it sounds. It is one layer over the page, one rule that the pen draws and the hand does not, and a memory that ties each mark to the words beneath it.

Given that layer, the old habits return on their own. I circle what puzzles me. I underline what persuades me. Late at night I draw the pen along the line and let it fade behind me, because the point was never the mark. The point was the attention the mark required.

My grandmother would have understood the tablet immediately. She would have asked for a pen and started arguing with it.
